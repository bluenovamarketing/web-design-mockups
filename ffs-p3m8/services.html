Where to begin

Not sure which one your child needs?

Some families arrive with a diagnosis and a referral already in hand. Others have only a feeling that something is harder for their child than it should be, and no idea which door to knock on. Both are good reasons to call.

Occupational therapy addresses the physical, sensory and organizational skills a child uses to take part in daily life: handwriting, coordination, sensory tolerance, emotional regulation, dressing, feeding and the stamina to sit through a school day. Speech therapy addresses the ability to be understood and to understand: pronunciation, vocabulary, sentence structure, following directions and holding a conversation.

There is honest overlap between them. Feeding difficulties can be sensory, motor, or both. A child who avoids group work may be struggling with language, or with regulation, or with both at once. If you are unsure, say so when you call. A short conversation usually narrows it down, and the evaluation settles it.

Many children work with one discipline. When a child benefits from both, the therapists coordinate so you are managing one plan and one set of goals rather than two.
